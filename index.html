How to use this — tap to hide or show
  1. Start with a photo: tap Import photo to pick one, or Take photo to shoot one. It fits inside the frame without stretching.
  2. Set the frame shape: pick Portrait or Landscape, then a paper size (9:16, 4:5, 1:1, 4:3, 16:9, A4). This only changes the frame shape.
  3. Use the grid to see proportions: Divisions sets how many boxes across, Labels turns the A, B, C / 1, 2, 3 letters on or off, and color + opacity make the grid easier to see over your photo.
  4. Find the perspective: pick 1-point, 2-point, or 3-point. Drag the green Horizon handle up or down — that line is your eye level. Drag the blue VP handles to where the parallel lines in your photo would meet. A VP can go off the edge of the photo on purpose; that is how real vanishing points work.
  5. Draw, then finish: sketch over the photo with the pencil, tap Lock when you are happy so nothing moves by accident, Zen mode hides everything but the picture, and Export PNG saves photo + grid + guides + sketch as one picture.

Lesson 1 — Tint vs Shade

A tint is your colour plus white. A shade is your colour plus black. They are not the same kind of change, and that is why they look different.

This is your base colour, untouched. Drag either way to see the why.

Why it changes: white bounces more light back, so a tint reads lighter and softer. Black soaks light up, so a shade reads darker and heavier. That is the whole trick — you are changing how much light comes back to the eye.

Lesson 2 — The acrylic truth, and its cousins

Acrylic dries darker. So mix a touch lighter than the colour you actually want, on purpose. If you match it wet, it will land darker than you planned once it dries. This is the one that trips up almost everybody at first.

Watercolour goes the other way: it dries lighter, because the water leaves and the pigment sits thinner on the paper. Paint it a touch darker than your target.

Gouache dries matte and flatter: it loses some of its wet shine and can read a little lighter and duller dry. Judge a gouache colour once it is dry, not while it is still wet on the palette.

Lesson 3 — Warm advances, cool recedes

Pick a colour and watch what distance does to it. Near things show the colour at full strength, warm and saturated. Far things sit behind air, so they come back lighter, cooler, and duller. That is not a style choice — it is how eyes read depth.

The rule, plain: the farther it is, the lighter, cooler, and duller it gets. Use that when you choose a palette for a scene with depth — do not paint the far hills the same colour as the near tree.

Lesson 4 — Horizon = eye level

The horizon line is not where the sky meets the earth. It is your eye level. Stand up and it rises; crouch down and it drops. Everything in a level scene points at it.

  • Every vanishing point for a level scene sits on the horizon. A point floating off it means the drawing mixed two viewpoints.
  • Farther things get smaller and closer to the horizon line.
  • Where the horizon cuts a person tells you where the viewer stood — waist height means standing, knee height means low down.

Lesson 5 — When and where a colour belongs

Choosing a colour is really choosing a job for it. Ask where it sits, what light it is in, and what feeling it has to carry — then modify it for that job.

  • Foreground needs warmth, saturation, and contrast. That is what pulls the eye forward.
  • Background needs cool, light, and dull. That is what lets it fall back.
  • Light warms a colour; shadow cools it and dulls it. Do not just add black for a shadow — shift it cooler too.

Lesson 6 — Studio truths

The little things people rarely say out loud, but working painters live by.

  • Never mix complements 50/50. Equal parts of opposite colours cancel each other into dull brown-grey. Use one to dominate (about 80%) and the other as a small accent.
  • Test a mixed colour on scrap first. What looks right on the palette can read completely different on the paper or canvas, especially once it dries.
  • Dark colours read darker in a large area. A dark swatch that looks fine small will feel much heavier across a whole wall or sky. Mix it a touch lighter than you think.
  • One dominant colour, the rest accents. Pick one colour to lead and let the others support it. Two colours fighting for the lead is how a painting gets noisy.
  • Squint at your work. Squinting kills the detail and leaves the big value shapes. If the picture still reads when squinted, the values are right.
